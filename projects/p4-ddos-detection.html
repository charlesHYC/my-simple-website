In-Network DDoS Detection and Dynamic Mitigation on a P4 Programmable Switch with Count-Min Sketch

Presented at the Kansai University Internship and Exchange Program 2026, Research Results Presentation. Department of Electrical Engineering, Chung Yuan Christian University.

P4 BMv2 P4Runtime gRPC Python Mininet Count-Min Sketch

The problem

A distributed denial-of-service attack buries a victim under traffic from many sources at once. The conventional defence mirrors traffic to a separate appliance to be measured, which costs money and, worse, costs time: the decision arrives well after the packets did.

A programmable switch can do the measuring itself. P4 lets the data plane keep per-source statistics and take a first-cut decision at line rate, the moment a packet passes through. This work builds the whole loop on one: detect, decide, mitigate, and release again on its own.

How it works

The work is split between the two planes. The data plane keeps a Count-Min Sketch of every source IP's packet rate and enforces a blocklist, both per packet at line rate. When a source starts to look suspicious it raises a digest to the controller. The controller, in Python over P4Runtime, is the only part that does the expensive thinking.

The sketch is three hash rows of 512 cells, hashed with crc16, crc32 and xor16. Every packet from a source adds one to its three cells; a query reads the same three and takes the smallest. Many addresses share only 512 cells, so two can land together, but a collision can only inflate a counter, never shrink it, and taking the minimum throws the inflated ones away. The size follows the Count-Min Sketch error bound: at ε = 0.01 and δ = 0.05 the bound asks for 272 cells per row, rounded up to the next power of two.

Two sketches alternate on a five-second ping-pong: one is written while the other is read, so a window is never counted and cleared at the same moment. A source is judged against its own EWMA baseline rather than a fixed ratio, which is what fails at small scale, and only the sources the data plane has already flagged reach that check. Two anomalous windows in a row install the drop rule; it lifts itself when the TTL runs out.

Results

From the flood starting to the attacker being blocked

End to end in Mininet, an attacker floods the victim at around 20 packets per second while a normal user sends background traffic. The attacker is caught and blocked within two windows; from then on every one of its packets is dropped and the normal user stays at zero loss. When the flood stops, the rule expires and the link comes back on its own.

The remaining 1.4 per cent that get through are low-and-slow attacks that stay just under the source's own baseline. That is not a tuning failure but a structural blind spot: the same baseline that keeps the false-positive rate at zero is what a patient attacker hides beneath. Quantifying it was part of the point.

What is borrowed and what is mine

Borrowed: the Count-Min Sketch, and Jaqen's split of "the data plane detects, the control plane decides". It sits alongside in-network measurement work such as HashPipe and Elastic Sketch, and switch-based defences such as Poseidon.

Mine: the ping-pong windowing, the per-source EWMA baseline in place of a ratio threshold, digest-driven candidate discovery so the expensive check only runs on suspects, the cold-start bias fix, the quantified low-and-slow blind spot, and the BMv2 digest and Thrift plumbing that makes it run.
